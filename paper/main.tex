\title{When There Is No Right Answer:\\Evaluation Failure and a Sampling-Scale
Fix for Low-Resolution Pixel-Art Textures}

\author{Anonymous}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Low-resolution pixel-art texture generation exposes a blind spot in how
generative models are evaluated. When the output is a $16\times16$ grid of
discrete colour cells, independent artists drawing the \emph{same} material
agree on 9.8\% of cells against 8.6\% expected under a paired null
(50{,}753 artist pairs) --- there is no unique right answer. We show this
single fact breaks both mainstream automatic evaluation routes. Per-pixel
metrics measure marginal fit rather than quality. And VLM judges fail in ways
that per-item agreement cannot detect: on the same human-labelled set, two
judges agree with the human on \emph{identical} fractions of items
(66.7\%), yet one compresses an 86\% effect to 62\% ($p{=}0.14$) and erases
its stratification while the other preserves both; a third inverts the human
conclusion outright. Under human blind comparison a trivial downsampling
baseline beats a purpose-built masked-prediction model 83:17. The bottleneck
is not model capacity but a \emph{structural-unit convention} mismatch: the
generator draws ${\sim}25$ units per tile regardless of canvas while artists
draw ${\sim}4.5$, measured independently from 5{,}979 artist tiles and 125
artist sources. Cropping to the detected dominant period fixes it, and
manipulating the unit count produces a monotone dose--response in the fix's
benefit (88\%, 54\%, 17\% at 27.7, 13.8 and 9.4 units). We pre-registered and
\emph{falsified} a pure sampling-limit account along the way; reporting that
failure is what selects the surviving mechanism.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The task sounds small. Given a solid-colour region and a material word ---
``brick'', ``wood'' --- produce a pixel-art texture for it at $16\times16$,
$24\times24$ or $32\times32$. But shrinking the canvas does something a
larger canvas hides: it makes the space of acceptable answers large relative
to the space of possible ones, and, unusually, it makes the degree of
non-uniqueness \emph{exactly computable}. A tile is a small grid of discrete
palette indices, so two artists' renditions of the same material either place
the same index in a cell or they do not, with nothing to interpolate and no
perceptual metric standing in between.

Measured that way, independent artists agree on $9.8\%$ of cells against
$8.6\%$ expected under a matched null (\S\ref{sec:agreement}). Whatever a
per-pixel loss is fitting on this task, it is not something artists agree
about. This single fact is the paper's hinge, and it disqualifies both of the
routes normally taken to evaluate a generative model here.

\paragraph{Both automatic evaluation routes fail, for the same reason.}
Reference-based quantities have almost no signal to work with, so they reduce
to measuring marginal fit. We watched the consequence before we understood
it: across three rounds of architecture changes, validation accuracy climbed
while the sample galleries did not visibly change (\S\ref{sec:noright}). The
other route, a VLM judge, fails differently and in a way the literature's
standard diagnostic cannot see. Two frontier judges agreed with human labels
on \emph{exactly} the same fraction of items, $66.7\%$, and one preserved the
human conclusion while the other destroyed it (\S\ref{sec:judges}). Per-item
agreement is not what makes a judge usable; reproducing a conclusion humans
have already established is.

\paragraph{Under valid evaluation, a trivial baseline wins.} Once the arbiter
is a human blind comparison, the ordering our automatic quantities had
reported inverts. Plain downsampling of a suitable high-resolution source
beats our own purpose-built masked-prediction model $83{:}17$ in a
pre-registered study, with the loss concentrated exactly where our
hand-designed structural priors act (\S\ref{sec:baseline}). Against
artist-drawn tiles the same baseline is indistinguishable ($43\%$,
$p{=}0.68$). This overturns the premise the project began from --- that
low-resolution pixel art is an artist's redesign rather than a downsampling
--- and it means a method has to earn its place somewhere other than where we
had been looking.

\paragraph{The bottleneck is a scale convention, not capacity.} Where no
suitable source exists, the source has to be generated, and there the
baseline is weak. Not because the generator cannot draw the material: its
$1024$px render is structurally sound. It draws about $25$ structural units
per tile while pixel artists place about $4.5$, a mismatch above five-fold
that we measure from two independent sources (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}).
Cropping the render to its detected dominant period repairs it. We
pre-registered a pure sampling-limit explanation of why this works and
\emph{falsified} it; reporting that failure is what selects the surviving
account, which then passed a second pre-registered test with a monotone
dose--response in unit count (\S\ref{sec:falsify}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Diagnosis.} An exactly computable inter-author agreement test,
  with the paired null it requires, showing this task has no unique
  reference; and evidence that per-pixel metrics and VLM judges both fail on
  it (\S\ref{sec:noright}).
\item \textbf{Baseline calibration.} Pre-registered human blind studies
  showing a trivial baseline beats a purpose-built method and matches human
  artists (\S\ref{sec:baseline}).
\item \textbf{Mechanism and fix.} Measurement of the structural-unit
  convention mismatch, a period-based cropping rule with a data-derived
  applicability gate validated on held-out materials, and pre-registered
  causal evidence with a dose--response (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}).
\item \textbf{Evaluation methodology.} A judge-admissibility protocol:
  validate on conclusion reproduction rather than per-item agreement, and let
  the judge's measured bias direction determine which claims it can support
  (\S\ref{sec:judges}).
\end{itemize}


\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Pixel-art generation.} The closest work is
SD-$\pi$XL~\citep{binninger2024sdpixl}, which produces palette-constrained
low-resolution imagery from an input image via score distillation. We
reproduced it; two properties matter here. It has no region-constraint
channel, so it cannot be asked to paint only inside a given shape, and
raising its conditioning strength degrades rather than improves the output.
More recent palette-aware diffusion models report FID~\citep{heusel2017fid},
palette-consistency and prompt-alignment scores alongside human preference.
Our \S\ref{sec:noright} questions the first family of numbers on this task
family: reference-based quantities presuppose a reference, and here the
per-cell agreement between independent authors of the same material is
$0.098$ against a paired null of $0.086$. There is no unique reference to
measure distance from.

\paragraph{Structure-preserving downscaling.}
\citet{kopf2013downscaling} optimise the shape and position of sampling
kernels to align with local features, and \citet{oztireli2015downscaling}
cast downscaling as optimisation against an SSIM~\citep{wang2004ssim}
objective. This line asks how to lose the least structure at a given scale
factor. Our diagnosis sits upstream of it: when the source packs ${\sim}25$
structural units into a 16-cell output, no kernel can represent them, and we
confirm that five downsamplers fail identically on the same renders
(\S\ref{sec:mechanism}). The remedy has to act on the scale factor itself.

\paragraph{{VLM}-as-judge and its biases.} Automatic judging with strong
models is now standard~\citep{zheng2023mtbench}, and positional bias is a
known failure~\citep{wang2024fair}. Recent benchmarks quantify it carefully:
\citet{webdevjudge2025} report order-swap consistency of $83.5$--$89.6\%$
across judges on web-development comparisons, with first-position preference
between $0.9\%$ and $15.8\%$, and argue the bias reflects a model deficiency
rather than annotation ambiguity, since explicit instructions do not remove
it. Our measurements on pixel-art comparisons are consistent with that
account and add a distinct, more consequential failure
(\S\ref{sec:judges}): judges that look equally good on per-item agreement
with humans differ completely in whether they reproduce the human
\emph{conclusion}. We argue this conclusion-level validation, not per-item
agreement, is what licenses using a judge.

\paragraph{Evaluation when the reference is not unique.} Independent redraws
of the same subject --- icon themes, emoji sets, texture packs --- are a
natural testbed for reference-scarce evaluation, but the degree of
non-uniqueness is usually not measurable. A discrete palette on a small grid
makes it exactly computable, and we contribute that test, together with the
paired null it requires, as a task-level diagnostic to run \emph{before}
choosing a metric.


\section{There Is No Right Answer}
\label{sec:noright}

\subsection{Independent artists barely agree on individual cells}
\label{sec:agreement}

A $16\times16$ tile drawn from a discrete palette makes agreement exactly
computable: two renditions of the same material either place the same palette
index in a cell or they do not. We collected 4{,}951 such tiles covering 517
materials from 64 independently authored texture packs, and compared every
pair of tiles depicting the same material --- 50{,}753 pairs.

The comparison needs a null, and the choice of null is where this measurement
is easiest to get wrong. Comparing artist $A$'s tile against a shuffle of
\emph{its own} cells has expectation $\sum_i p_A(i)^2$, whereas the real
comparison $A$ against $B$ has expectation $\sum_i p_A(i)p_B(i)$; when two
artists use their palettes differently the second is necessarily smaller by
Cauchy--Schwarz. That null sits systematically high, and against it artists
appear to agree \emph{below} chance. We therefore use a paired null: shuffle
$B$'s cells and compare $A$ against the shuffle, preserving $B$'s own marginal
while destroying its spatial arrangement, so both sides pair the same
marginals.

Under the paired null, artists agree on $0.0977$ of cells against $0.0859$
expected (Figure~\ref{fig:agreement}) --- $1.2$ percentage points above
chance, significant only because $n$ is large, and a third of artist pairs
($33.5\%$) fall \emph{below} their own null. Per-cell agreement between
independent artists carries almost no signal.

\paragraph{The finding is not specific to pixel art.} We repeated the identical
protocol on three independently designed emoji sets (Noto, Twemoji, OpenMoji;
63 emoji, 189 set pairs). Measured over all cells, emoji redraws appear far
more consistent, $+0.1367$ over the null against textures' $+0.0117$. But
39.8\% of emoji cells are transparent background composited to white and agree
trivially. Restricted to foreground, the gap is $+0.0130$ --- essentially the
same as textures. Measuring this kind of agreement without excluding regions
that agree trivially inflates it by an order of magnitude.

\subsection{Per-pixel objectives therefore measure marginal fit}

If the conditional distribution over tiles is this wide, a per-cell loss
against one artist's specific tile cannot separate a good sample from a bad
one beyond how well it matches the marginal. We observed the consequence
directly before we understood it: across three rounds of architecture changes
to a masked-prediction model, validation accuracy rose steadily while the
generated galleries did not visibly change. Every decision in that phase
rested on a quantity that could not see what we cared about.

\subsection{VLM judges fail, and per-item agreement cannot detect it}
\label{sec:judges}

We replayed a completed human blind study (72 pairs, \S\ref{sec:baseline}) to
three frontier judges under an identical protocol, asking each pair in both
orders and discarding disagreements. Order swapping is not optional here:
before debiasing, judges chose the left image 67\% of the time, an order of
magnitude beyond the ${\sim}5\%$ reported elsewhere, and the same 67\% appears
on emoji pairs, so it is not specific to this content.

The results (Figure~\ref{fig:judges}) separate into three failure modes.
\texttt{gemini-3.1-pro} compresses the human's 87\% baseline win rate to 62\%
($p{=}0.14$) and flattens the stratification that carried the study's main
finding, reporting 37\% where the human measured 6\%.
\texttt{gpt-5.6-sol} inverts it: 42\% against the human's 78\%, and 62\% on
the stratum where the human measured 4\%. \texttt{claude-opus-5} preserves
direction, significance and stratum ordering (73\%, $p{=}0.008$; 25\% versus
33\%) while still compressing.

The methodological point is panel (c). \texttt{gemini-3.1-pro} and
\texttt{claude-opus-5} agree with the human on \emph{exactly} the same
fraction of items, 66.7\%, and one destroys the conclusion while the other
keeps it. Per-item agreement --- the quantity this literature reports --- is
not evidence that a judge can be used. A judge must be validated on whether it
reproduces a conclusion already established by humans.

\paragraph{A judge's bias direction determines what it can support.} Because
compression pulls any real difference toward 50\%, a compressing judge
manufactures null results. It can therefore support the existence of a large
effect, as a lower bound, and can refute a null by finding significance in
spite of compressing --- but it cannot confirm a null. We apply that rule
below rather than treating a validated judge as a general substitute for
annotation.

\section{A Trivial Baseline Is Enough}
\label{sec:baseline}

If per-pixel objectives measure the wrong thing (\S\ref{sec:agreement}) and
automatic judges cannot be trusted without validation (\S\ref{sec:judges}),
then the only admissible arbiter left is a human blind comparison. Run under
that arbiter, the ordering between a trivial baseline and a specialised model
inverts relative to what every automatic quantity had reported.

\subsection{The specialised model loses to the baseline}

Our own three rounds of architecture work produced a mask-prediction model
with hand-designed structural priors --- brick period, border treatment,
running-bond phase offset, per-material gating. We pre-registered a blind
comparison against the trivial downsampling baseline: 72 pairs with five
attention checks, all passed, each item verified against the pipeline that
produced it.

The baseline won $49{:}10$, $83\%$, $p<0.001$. Worse, the loss concentrates
exactly where the priors act. On the seeded stratum --- items where a
structural prior actually fired --- the model won $2/40$, $5\%$; on the
unseeded control stratum it won $8/19$, $42\%$ ($p{=}0.65$), close to a tie.
The stratum difference is Fisher $p{=}0.001$, and its \emph{direction is the
opposite} of what we pre-registered: we had written that if the priors work,
the seeded stratum should score higher. Win rate falls monotonically with
structure score, $40\% \rightarrow 11\% \rightarrow 0\%$ ($\rho{=}-0.509$,
$p{=}3.8\times10^{-5}$). An earlier comparison of the same model without
priors lost to the baseline at $60\%$; adding the priors moved that to $83\%$.
The priors did not fail to help --- they actively hurt.

\paragraph{The structural ruler ranked them backwards.} We had also built a
structural-descriptor distance to serve as a cheap quality proxy. It passed
three validation criteria and was split-half stable, and it scored the seeded
condition better than the unseeded one ($0.93$ vs $1.74$) --- the reverse of
the human ordering ($5\%$ vs $42\%$). The criteria established that the
descriptor can tell structured content from unstructured content, which is a
different question from whether a tile reads as the right material. Building
the ruler succeeded; treating it as a stand-in for quality was the error. Two
earlier conflicts between this ruler and visual inspection, resolved both
times in favour of the eye, are retrospectively vindicated.

\subsection{Against artists, the baseline is indistinguishable}

The baseline beating our model would be uninteresting if the baseline were
merely a low bar. It is not. Given a suitable high-resolution source, 27 pairs
(24 comparisons plus three attention checks, all passed; median 1.4\,s per
item) put artist-drawn tiles against the downsampled baseline. Artists were
preferred on $10/23 = 43\%$, $p{=}0.68$ --- if anything slightly behind. The
result holds in both strata (geometric $50\%$, non-geometric $36\%$, Fisher
$p{=}0.68$) and structure score does not predict the outcome
($\rho{=}-0.007$, $p{=}0.98$).

This was the third pre-registered outcome for that comparison, and the one we
had least expected: it says the baseline is already at parity, so there is no
room for a method to work in. It also overturns the premise the project
started from --- that low-resolution pixel art is an artist's redesign rather
than a downsampling. An earlier attempt to test that premise with automatic
features had been declared falsified and then withdrawn on the grounds that
the ruler was blind to differences the eye could see; that escape is not
available here, because the arbiter is the eye.

\paragraph{What this comparison can and cannot support.} With $n{=}24$, power
against a true $65\%$ artist preference is only $21\%$, so the null is not
confirmed --- the honest reading is that no large effect is present. A 60-pair
extension (power $56\%$ at $65\%$, $84\%$ at $70\%$) is prepared and awaiting
labels. Both this study and the pre-registered comparison above were labelled
by a single annotator, which we return to in \S\ref{sec:limitations}.


\section{The Real Bottleneck: Structural-Unit Convention}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\subsection{The failure is upstream of the downsampling kernel}

Given a curated high-resolution source, the trivial baseline is already
adequate (\S\ref{sec:baseline}). The interesting case is a material that does
not exist yet, where the source must be generated. There, a text-to-image
render at $1024$px looks structurally sound --- a mossy brick wall with
well-defined courses and mortar --- and yet every standard downsampling kernel
turns it to mush at $16\times16$. We tried five (box, median, extremum,
contrast-weighted, bimodal); all five fail identically on the same renders, so
the kernel is not the variable.

Counting the structure explains it. The render packs roughly 25 brick courses
into what must become 16 output cells. Improving the kernel cannot help,
which is precisely where work on content-adaptive downscaling operates.

\subsection{Artists hold a fixed number of units per tile}

Measuring the dominant period of 5{,}979 artist tiles across three sizes gives
median periods of $4.0$, $7.0$ and $7.0$ pixels at $16$, $32$ and $64$ ---
that is $4.0$, $4.6$ and $9.1$ structural units per tile. Between $16$ and
$32$ artists hold the unit count near $4.5$ and enlarge the units rather than
adding more; only at $64$ do they add units.

A second, independent measurement agrees. Across 125 artist-authored
high-resolution sources with a detected directional period, a period spans
$4.80$ output cells once downsampled to $16$ (IQR $3.84$--$7.17$), with only
12\% below the Nyquist limit. The same renders that fail above span $0.73$ to
$0.95$ --- past the limit by a factor of five. This is why curated sources
need no intervention and generated ones do.

\subsection{Fix and applicability}

We crop the source to a window of side $\text{period}\times(\text{size}/4.5)$
around the detected dominant period, then downsample. Two conditions gate it:
a period must be detected, and normalised anisotropy must reach $0.20$.
The anisotropy condition is necessary because period detection runs on row and
column profiles and is meaningless for isotropic content. Across all 4{,}951
artist tiles, median normalised anisotropy is $0.287$ where a directional
period is detected and $0.063$ where it is not; contrasting directional
material classes (planks, brick, stone brick) against isotropic ones (foliage,
gravel, metal blocks) separates them by more than an order of magnitude,
$1.090$ against $0.032$. The threshold sits between the two groups either way. Together the conditions move hit rate from 70\% to 90\% and false
triggers from 35\% to 6\% on the materials used to set them, and generalise to
a disjoint held-out set at 93\% and 16\%.

The gate fires on roughly half of prompts. The correct statement is therefore
that the method wins \emph{within its applicability}, which covers about half
of the materials we tested, and changes nothing elsewhere. Judged by
\texttt{claude-opus-5} --- the only judge that passed \S\ref{sec:judges}'s
validation --- the crop is preferred on $31/35$ pairs, $89\%$,
$p{=}3.5\times10^{-6}$, Jeffreys interval $[75\%, 96\%]$. Because that judge
compresses, this is a lower bound. \pending{human blind study, 39 pairs}

\subsection{A pre-registered falsification selects the mechanism}
\label{sec:falsify}

The obvious reading of \S\ref{sec:mechanism} is a sampling-limit account:
structure finer than the output grid cannot be represented, so cropping
restores what Nyquist removed. That account makes a prediction --- as output
resolution grows, more structure survives uncropped and cropping should matter
less --- and we pre-registered it over five output sizes with fixed criteria
before running.

It failed (Figure~
ef{fig:gradient}). Across two independent protocols the trend is
$\rho{=}-0.137$ ($p{=}0.277$) and $\rho{=}+0.044$ ($p{=}0.64$), and at 48px
the crop wins 100\% against 88\% at 16px, triggering the pre-registered
falsification condition. At 64px a 47px source period already spans 2.9 output
cells, comfortably above Nyquist, and cropping still wins 79--90\%.

What survives is the convention account: the generator draws ${\sim}25$ units
per tile \emph{regardless of output size}, artists draw ${\sim}4.5$, and the
implied crop factor $25/4.5\approx5.6$ matches the $1/4.2$ to $1/5.4$ we
measure. Because that mismatch does not depend on output resolution, this
account predicts no gradient --- which is what we observe. The failed
prediction is what separates the two; had the gradient appeared, both would
have been compatible with it.

\subsection{Manipulating the unit count changes the benefit}

We then tested the surviving account's own prediction. Prompt-level levers
turned out to be inert: three variants failed a paired manipulation check on
rendered period ($p{=}0.38$, $0.19$, $1.00$), and an earlier attempt had
passed a check we had written too loosely, which we report as a void test
rather than a result. Render resolution does move it: at 384px the unit count
falls from $32.5$ to $9.3$ ($-71.5\%$, $p{=}0.0062$), clearing a
manipulation criterion fixed in advance, while 512px did not clear it and was
excluded from the pre-registered test.

The prediction held. At 384px the crop is preferred on only $17\%$ of pairs
against $88\%$ in the control, a drop of $71.7$ points
($p{=}6.3\times10^{-9}$), and the direction survives all three treatments of
order-swap disagreements. Adding 512px as a descriptive midpoint yields a
monotone dose--response (Figure~\ref{fig:dose}): $27.7$, $13.8$ and $9.4$
units map to $88\%$, $54\%$ and $17\%$, with the midpoint sitting at no effect
($p{=}0.749$).

\paragraph{The effect is condition-level, not per-image.} Within a condition,
per-image unit count does not predict the outcome
($\rho{=}+0.098$, $p{=}0.316$, $n{=}106$), because unit count there is
confounded with material type. What is actionable is a setting-level rule ---
crop when rendering large, do not when rendering small --- not a per-image
gate. Our gate still fires on 48\% of prompts at 384px where cropping hurts;
we report that as a limitation rather than fitting a unit-count threshold the
per-image data do not support.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{One annotator.} Every human blind comparison we report --- the
72-pair pre-registered study and the 24-pair artist comparison --- was
labelled by a single person, an author. Attention checks passed and items were
verified against their pipelines, but inter-annotator reliability is unmeasured
and the labeller was not blind to the project's hypotheses, only to which
condition produced each image.

\paragraph{A null we cannot confirm.} The artist comparison is underpowered
against the alternative that matters: at $n{=}24$, a true $65\%$ artist
preference would be detected only $21\%$ of the time. ``Indistinguishable''
here means no large effect, not equivalence. The same asymmetry constrains
every judge-mediated result: because our validated judge compresses effects,
its numbers support lower bounds and can refute nulls, but cannot establish
them (\S\ref{sec:judges}).

\paragraph{Conditional, not general, wins.} The crop applies only where its
two gate conditions hold, which is roughly half of the prompts we tested. Its
$89\%$ preference is a within-applicability figure from a compressing judge,
so it is a lower bound over a restricted domain; the corresponding human study
is prepared but unlabelled. We report no aggregate win rate over all prompts
because there is no defensible way to score the cases the method declines.

\paragraph{The gate does not follow from the effect.} The dose--response in
\S\ref{sec:mechanism} is condition-level, and within a condition per-image
unit count does not predict the outcome ($p{=}0.316$). Our gate consequently
misfires: at 384px it still triggers on $48\%$ of prompts, where cropping
hurts. Fitting a unit-count threshold would be unsupported by the per-image
data, so the practical rule remains a setting-level one, and a per-image gate
is open.

\paragraph{One generator, two domains.} The ${\sim}25$ structural units per
tile is measured on SDXL renders. Whether the convention mismatch takes the
same magnitude --- or the same form --- in other text-to-image models is
untested, and a model whose convention already matched would need no crop.
The agreement finding replicates on emoji, but both domains are small-canvas
raster redrawing by human authors; we do not claim it for reference-scarce
tasks in general.
